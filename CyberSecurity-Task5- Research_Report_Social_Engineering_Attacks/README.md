# Social Engineering Attacks Research Report

## Project Overview

Social engineering remains one of the most effective methods used by attackers to compromise organizations, not because of weak technology, but because it targets human trust, urgency, and helpfulness. Even organizations with strong firewalls, encryption, and endpoint protection can be breached through a single convincing phone call, email, or planted USB drive, since these attacks bypass technical controls entirely by manipulating the person operating the system. This project researches and documents the most common social engineering techniques, explains how each one works in practice step by step, and grounds every threat in a real, documented incident rather than a hypothetical scenario. The goal is to provide a clear, professional reference that shows how attackers manipulate people rather than systems, and how organizations can build layered defenses, spanning training, procedure, and technology, to reduce that risk.

## Objective

This project was completed as part of the **Oasis Infobyte Cyber Security Internship – Task 5**, with the goal of producing a well-researched, professionally documented report on social engineering threats suitable for a technical portfolio.

## Features

- Comprehensive research report covering four major social engineering techniques, from phishing to quid pro quo
- Real-world case studies, including the 2011 RSA SecurID spear-phishing breach, the 2023 MGM Resorts help desk compromise, and the Stuxnet USB attack on Iran's Natanz facility
- Step-by-step breakdowns of how each attack unfolds, from initial reconnaissance to final exploitation
- Detailed prevention and mitigation strategies for every threat, written to be practical rather than purely theoretical
- A comparison table summarizing attack vectors, primary targets, exploited psychological levers, and the most effective countermeasure for each threat type
- Organizational recommendations, including a five-point Employee Security Awareness Training Checklist
- Credible, authoritative references drawn from recognized cybersecurity institutions rather than blogs or crowdsourced sources

## Conclusion

Social engineering succeeds because it exploits people, not systems, which means no firewall or encryption standard can fully prevent it on its own. Defending against it requires a layered approach: continuous employee awareness training, strict identity-verification procedures, multi-factor authentication, and a culture where reporting suspicious activity is simple and encouraged. This project reflects that layered mindset, translating documented real-world attacks into practical lessons for building a more resilient organization.
